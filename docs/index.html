Abstract
A small ground robot can carry one camera and a few watts of compute, but the depth foundation models that could turn that camera into a range sensor are vision transformers sized for GPUs. We bring one to a Raspberry Pi 5 with the Hailo-8L, an accelerator that runs every layer in integer arithmetic, attention included. Quantised for it, Depth Anything V2 loses large parts of the scene, and we measure why: at 449 tokens, 91% of softmax weights fall below one int8 step while carrying 17-33% of the attention mass. Our answer is to distil, not quantise. An attention-free ResNet-34 U-Net student of Depth Anything V2-Large (24.5M parameters) runs at 27.1 FPS on the chip, its int8 output matches its float twin (Pearson 0.99993), and on held-out scenes of its simulated yard it is the most accurate depth network on the chip (AbsRel 0.269 against 0.354-0.530). On 1,000 stereo-verified points of a real outdoor drive it is not resolvably different from the vendor's Depth Anything V2-Small build, while running faster (27.1 vs 21.6 FPS). Ranging obstacles by where they meet the floor needs only the camera height, so mapping, hybrid-A* planning and SLAM run on the Pi's own CPU. In real-time hardware-in-the-loop tests in a Gaussian-splat reconstruction of a real yard, the stack reaches the goal without contact in 7.9$\pm$1.1 of 10 scenes, against 9.2$\pm$0.4 with exact obstacles, and in 7.7$\pm$0.7 on its own pose with simulated wheel odometry (ten seeds each; 8.2$\pm$0.4 with the real Pi and Hailo-8L in the loop, five seeds). The source code, pre-trained models and data are available at https://github.com/itaim18/DANIEL.

AbsPDFDawid J. Kopiczko, Tijmen Blankevoort, Yuki M. Asano · First 2026-11-24 · Latest 2026-11-24
Abstract
Reasoning models become long-horizon agents through reinforcement learning or, more cheaply, through fine-tuning on a stronger teacher's trajectories, which contain the teacher's chain-of-thought as well as its actions. The standard fine-tuning approach imitates both, yet the strongest teachers are closed models whose chain-of-thought is hidden behind an API. We show that the chain-of-thought can be left out altogether and that the long-horizon policy can be distilled from the teacher's actions alone. The student is trained on these trajectories with its reasoning block left empty, while a KL loss on a replay buffer of its own generations limits drift from its initial model. Surprisingly, the policy learned from these trajectories with an empty reasoning block carries over to inference with thinking enabled, where the student reasons in its own style while acting like the teacher, an effect we call cross-format transfer. Distilling the actions of Qwen3.6-27B raises the task success of Qwen3-4B on AppWorld from 7% to 18% and that of Qwen3-32B from 12% to 25%. We further introduce CoT-proxies, short summaries of the teacher's reasoning placed after the empty reasoning block, which raise Qwen3-4B further, to 35%. Since neither actions nor CoT-proxies need the hidden chain-of-thought, the recipe applies unchanged to closed teachers. Distilling GPT-5.6 Luna from its visible outputs and the reasoning summaries its API returns raises Qwen3-4B from 7% to 48% on AppWorld and from 3% to 28% on SWE-bench Verified.

Abstract
A language-model decoder carries state between decoding steps, and in hardware a wrong state update can leave the current token correct and corrupt the output several tokens later, so a design can pass output tests and still be wrong. AIChipBench asks what LLM coding agents need to build such hardware correctly. Agents implement quantized Qwen3.5, Gemma 3, Gemma 4, and Llama 3.2 decoders in synthesizable register-transfer-level (RTL) code against an executable integer reference that specifies the outputs and the stored state at every decoding step. They debug against public tests under fixed budgets; their frozen designs are graded on held-out token sequences, resets, memory stalls, and synthesis, and a second track asks them to speed up a correct design at nearly fixed area. Abstract
Programmatic verifiers provide scalable reward signals for training coding agents with reinforcement learning, yet subtle harness defects and incomplete unit tests allow policies to earn full credit without solving the intended task. Abstract
We study the sample complexity of learning robust coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) in finite-horizon, general-sum, distributionally robust Markov games with known rewards and a generative model of the nominal transitions. Each player evaluates a policy by its worst-case return over total-variation (TV) balls centered at the nominal transition distribution of every stage, state, and joint action; this is the standard $(s,a)$-rectangular uncertainty set. Existing generative-model guarantees for this model scale with the number $\prod_iA_i$ of joint actions, where $A_i$ is the number of actions of player~$i$. Since this number grows exponentially with the number of players, these guarantees suffer from the curse of multiagency. We design an algorithm whose sample complexity depends on the action sets only through the largest individual action-set size $A_*:=\max_iA_i$. Two ingredients make this possible: a plug-in estimate of the robust backup whose mean error does not depend on the number of states, and a single batch of samples per round that serves as bandit feedback for all players at once.
