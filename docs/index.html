Abstract
Long-horizon agentic tasks require a model to call tools over many steps at contexts of 100K tokens or more, yet many low-bit quantization methods are calibrated on short web text and judged on perplexity. This mismatch is costly: existing quantizations of Qwen3.8-27B at or below 3 bits per weight match BF16 on shorter tasks like AIME, yet in published results solve at most 72% as many tasks as BF16 on DeepSWE, a long-horizon agentic benchmark. Abstract
Agents based on large language models (LLMs) can now delete files and send payments on behalf of their users. News media reports routinely describe failures of these agents (e.g., Replit's coding agent deleting a production database), but a report often only says what went wrong and rarely (in)validates the incident or finds its root cause. Incident catalogs collect these reports, and benchmarks record whether agents fail, but neither replicates an incident nor tests the cause its report might identify. In this paper, we present ROGUE-AI (Replication of Agentic Security Events: Audit and Investigation), a multi-agent framework that closes the above gap and rebuilds each reported incident offline together with a matched control that lacks only the blamed cause, and runs agents on both. Our results suggest that ROGUE-AI is able to reproduce the reported harm in 89% of its 108 attempted replications. Agents built on five current GPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek models made the reported mistake in 18% to 33% of runs, against at most 1% without the blamed cause. Of the 72 incidents with real-world harm, 62 had no underlying attacker, and in these, agents did what their task asked for but missed an ordinary hazard, such as a database shared with the live site. Replications without an attacker caused more harm than replications of prompt injection for all five models. Defenses against prompt injection look for injected instructions, but these replications contain none. Further, a warning after each tool output that names the hazard and its consequence lowered harm for all five models, whereas a note of the same length that does not mention the hazard left most of the harm in place. Built from real incidents, the ROGUE-AI replications form a reusable audit framework that vendors can rerun before each new release.

Abstract
In weight-space programming, a language model edits a network's weights directly, without gradients. We ask what a successful repair of this kind relies on. We plant faults in the weights of two vision-language models, SmolVLM-256M and LFM2-VL-450M, and check each edit made by a coding agent against the original weights. Without being told where to look, the agent finds rescaled projector rows and noise in the vision encoder, infers the unknown scale factors, and returns most or all of the rescaled weights to their exact original values and accuracy to near its original level. These repairs depend on a trace of how the faults were computed. The original weights have 16-bit precision, and faults computed in 32-bit arithmetic leave low bits that only the damaged weights carry, a cue that a real failure need not leave. When we round those bits away, each model answers our test questions as before, but the agent, unaided, no longer repairs either model: on LFM2-VL accuracy recovers by about 4 points, against 17 from the unrounded weights. Marking the damaged weights again in their low bits brings the edits back to them. Rounded weights still hold statistical evidence of the rescaling. On SmolVLM the agent uses it once a sentence in the prompt, which we wrote knowing the fault, names such a statistic; on LFM2-VL, whose scale factors are larger, most sessions find the rescaled rows unaided, but none finds the noise. Swapped rows or columns, which keep the set of stored values, are not found unaided on either model within our budgets. We recommend that studies of weight repair report the number format of the weights and include a rounded control.

Abstract
Studies of how long code from AI coding agents survives after it is merged reach conflicting verdicts: agent code is reported to be retained longer than human code, about as long, or modified sooner. Each study makes defensible measurement choices, but how much these choices decide the verdict is unknown. We revisit the survival of agent-attributed code in our earlier study: 854,139 lines that 2,802 merged pull requests (PRs) added to 215 repositories of the AIDev dataset. We vary nine measurement choices in five research questions and build a specification curve of 88 survival analyses, tested under three variance estimators, several observation windows, and permuted labels. Humans make most modifications to agent code under every unit and window. Re-run on our data, five published designs each change their result with a single choice. Whether agent code survives longer than human code is therefore decided as much by the measurement as by the code. We derive an operationalization checklist for post-merge studies.

Abstract
Every empirical analysis on observational data rests on dozens of defensible analytical choices ("forks") that its readers cannot see. We develop an agentic multiverse analysis (AMA) framework that makes a conclusion's sensitivity to those choices visible at scale, and we position it as a decision-support instrument for human experts. A pilot study first deploys 150 autonomous AI coding agents on one dataset and one set of research questions and finds that they disagree through the same defensible methodological choices human analysts make, though over a narrower range. AI peer review leaves their dispersion almost unchanged. Exposure to top-rated exemplar reports then collapses it, largely through imitation rather than analysis. Built on those lessons, AMA has agents enumerate a published paper's possible fork space, score every fork against a validity rubric, execute the validated specification grid, and cross-examine one another's work under strict separation of duties. Applied to ten empirical studies from a leading management journal's replication packages (1,755 specification estimates), the framework reproduces every paper's original estimates, and shows that some conclusions hold across every specification the assessment validates, while others are sensitive to individual analytical choices that the published texts do not discuss. The case studies suggest our AMA-based measures can serve as robustness proxies for empirical findings. AI makes multiverse analysis practical, where it once required resources few research teams can afford: our exercises take hours of agent time and tens of dollars per paper. We discuss the implications for replication policy and for AI-assisted peer review in information systems.
