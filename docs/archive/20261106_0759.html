Abstract
Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) extends on-policy distillation (OPD) to consolidate capabilities from multiple teachers. Although prior work reports near-perfect in-distribution (IND) distillation performance, we find that this can mask incomplete capability consolidation beyond the supervised distribution. A preliminary study on synthetic reasoning shows that OPD does not fully transfer teachers' generalization capabilities. We then extend this investigation to real-world tasks, evaluating extrapolative and compositional generalization across mathematical reasoning, coding, agent, and long-context capabilities using Qwen3 models. Across extrapolative settings, OPD and MOPD reach 96.04-102.73% of their teachers' IND scores. Yet their out-of-distribution (OOD) scores remain 2.45-16.25% below the base model's. Across three compositional settings, MOPD improves IND performance over the base model, but its performance on composed tasks as OOD remains 18.19-24.34% below that of the best teachers. To understand these gaps, we examine teacher capability and data coverage as two potential bottlenecks. Jointly alleviating these bottlenecks eliminates OOD forgetting in our experiments and suggests the potential for generalization beyond the teacher. (M)OPD also reduces OOD forgetting on average relative to its reinforcement learning (RL) baselines. Analysis of the associated update geometry reveals smaller and more spectrally concentrated updates on average than those of the RL baselines, along with greater shared update structure under MOPD. Motivated by these findings, we propose geometry-based patching and base-preserving replay to improve OOD performance while largely preserving IND performance. Our findings identify a blind spot in capability consolidation via (M)OPD, highlighting the need for algorithms that explicitly support OOD retention and generalization.

Abstract
Speculative decoding has been treated purely as a systems optimization that speeds up large language model decoding without changing the model behavior. Under this view, the growing effort to train better draft models has been focused solely on acceptance rate. We argue that this view does not hold true for long-horizon agent reasoning. Measuring frontier open-weight models on complex agentic tasks with different speculative decoding approaches, we found that numerical noise grows within verification windows, and the draft acceptance results of the thinking process can change the output distribution of the model after 30 steps, thus noticeably influencing the reasoning accuracy of long-horizon agents in random directions. In this work, we improve the reasoning capability of target models by controlling such noise. While we cannot change the overall token distribution verified by the target model, we can encode critical reasoning with highest precision by training the draft model to be rejected. We identify these points in agent traces and train the draft model to favor adversarial preference. At inference the trained draft and the target part ways precisely at these decision points, verification restarts, so that critical thinking tokens are sampled from the target model and encoded under its least noisy conditions. This approach makes critical reasoning better indexed and more durable over a long horizon. On complex agentic coding benchmarks, our approach raises end-to-end success rate with no change to the target model or its output distribution. Our results recast the speculative decoding mechanism from a pure accelerator into an active allocator for context precision that improves the accuracy of long-horizon agents by training 0.06% to 0.28% of hosted parameters.

Abstract
Teleoperation remains the default way to collect robot demonstrations, but every episode costs robot and operator time, and every new task starts from scratch. We ask whether a coding agent can take over the teleoperator's job. In our DataFarm, the agent receives a data request and one calibration episode of the robot in the target scene and rebuilds the task in simulation: it writes the scene, an expert controller, and a success judge. The simulator supplies exact actions, and a video model conditioned on real images renders each rollout as photographic video, generating all camera views together consistently. The output is real-ready data: synchronized camera views labelled with the robot's actions, on which a policy can be trained directly and deployed on the real robot. Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission. On five real-robot tasks, policies trained only on farm data match policies trained on as many teleoperated episodes (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials), and under an unseen tablecloth and coloured light they keep 87 successes where the teleoperation-trained policies fall to 46. Adapting a task is fully automatic and costs \$21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, plus \$5 for a render contract; every further episode costs \$0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, and review, with no robot or operator. One hour of three-view data for a task costs \$107 on average, these costs included, slightly below a quote of \$117 for teleoperation in China and below \$290 in labour alone in North America.

Abstract
Spec-driven development (SDD) asks a coding agent to work from specifications, plans, and task lists rather than from the chat transcript. We study one such workflow for Claude Code, in which almost every control is an instruction carried out by the same model that it constrains. The user must approve each stage before the next begins. A reviewer subagent checks each artefact against a fixed, stage-specific defect list, and an executor subagent may only touch the files listed in its task. After each block of tasks, the orchestrator runs a file-inventory check to detect undeclared changes. We analyse the logs and transcripts of nine features in seven projects, all run by the authors, ranging from a Fortran solver and Python programs to books and this paper. Of these, five finished with every planned task done, including the Fortran solver feature, and 288 of the 581 planned tasks were completed block by block. The executor kept to its declared files: it left only one undeclared change in the deliverable folder, and the check caught it, although its reports record other departures from the protocol. The weak point was the orchestrator, which runs and judges its own checks. It recorded undeclared changes in 11 of 247 block checks, mostly from the authors' in-round requests and the environment, and stopped work in only four.

Abstract
Existing implementations are usually maintained for most of their life and replaced only as an exception. Coding agents may change that. If agents can produce new implementations from requirements, tests, architectural constraints, and operational records that survive replacement, changing a system may no longer require modifying its existing codebase. We call this process regeneration. An implementation is disposable when regeneration becomes a routine alternative to maintenance. Regenerating and verifying a clean implementation from current requirements and tests may be cheaper than having agents navigate (and potentially compound) existing technical debt, and could eliminate much of that debt. Yet existing code may contain knowledge absent from the preserved artifacts, including safeguards introduced after failures and assumptions that were never documented. A regenerated implementation may pass the available tests and still lose behavior that matters in production. This leaves open how to establish continuity across replacements and whether regeneration reduces maintenance effort or shifts it to the artifacts and processes that support it. We examine these questions through an exploratory review of practitioner literature. Published examples show that agents can replace existing implementations, but do not establish that repeated regeneration is safe or economical. If implementations become disposable, what should software engineering preserve?

Abstract
We introduce Dory1.0-4B, a 4-billion-parameter looped (latent-recurrent) language model with elastic depth: a single checkpoint serves different inference compute budgets. Dory1.0-4B is built on a normalized Transformer (nGPT) backbone with interleaved sliding-window and full-attention layers, organized into an input block, a shared recurrent block, and an output block. Abstract

However, an agent may achieve a user's goal while causing unintended harm, such as deleting unrelated files, weakening access controls, or corrupting data, in response to benign inputs without explicit jailbreaking. This failure mode is particularly important because apparent task success may reassure users that an agent behaved correctly, causing harmful side effects to go unnoticed. Despite its importance, harmful success is difficult to measure because conventional task-success metrics overlook harmful behaviors, while existing safety evaluations are often designed solely to assess harmful outcomes. These results suggest that successful task completion can frequently coexist with unintended harm, and that task-success metrics alone may substantially understate the potential safety risks of coding agents.
